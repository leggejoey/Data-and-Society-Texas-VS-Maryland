Redistricting “fair” maps in Texas and Maryland

Ethan Erickson, Joey Legge, Cove Vandervort, Elysia Whichard

DAT 4500: Data and Society

Abstract

        The everpresent issue of redistricting provides an ongoing challenge in which parties struggle to gain the slimmest margins of an advantage, and the question of true “fair” representation is often forgotten in the wake of these battles for the political upper hand. Texas and Maryland are two states that have engaged in at least some part of the process of mid-decade redistricting (outside the usual every-10-year redrawing with the census), both of which have their own histories with gerrymandering and opposite political motivations when approaching this attempt at redrawing their states’ congressional districts. An analysis of redistricting in these two states was approached with the goal of generating “fair” districts; in this case, this means ensuring that districts are as compact as possible, drawn to respect community/political boundaries that already exist within the state, and strive for as close to proportional representation of minority groups as possible by generating districts that have a majority vote for these groups, correlating with the overall population of the state. Data was gathered from several sources, including ALARM data to analyze and generate new maps primarily using the redist_smc function, and the respective map for each state that was decided to be most “fair” was assessed on the above metrics for compactness, partisan distribution, and minority representation. This provides a foundation of understanding for the state of redistricting in Texas and Maryland, but cannot be guaranteed to be the most “fair” possibilities, due to limitations in the amount of maps that we able to be generated, different statistics that could have been computed to assess fairness, and of course the difficulty in producing an ultimate definition of fairness that can be agreed upon by enough people.

Introduction

Redistricting is the process of redrawing the boundaries of electoral districts within each state, both for the US congress as well as state legislative districts. This process normally occurs every ten years, as mandated, when the census updates, and is intended to ensure that a roughly equal number of people reside in each allocated district, to allow for equal representation across areas.

While initially intended with fairness in mind, the process of redistricting has been co-opted and corrupted over the entirety of its history, for a variety of reasons. The deliberate manipulation of the drawing of electoral boundaries for political reasons is called gerrymandering, and is, unfortunately, an integral and omnipresent part of U.S. history.

Gerrymandering originates from a person, Gov. Elbridge Gerry (one of the state governors of Massachusetts), who, during the 19th century (March 26, 1812), signed into law a bill that created a salamander-like shape (in Boston, Massachusetts) that caused an extreme imbalance of political power in favor of the Democratic-Republican party. Hence the name gerrymandering. Many states have since used the intentional manipulation of district boundaries to create unfair political power for whichever party was in power at the time.

        In our case, Texas and Maryland are the two states that will be focused on for this report. Texas especially has a long history of gerrymandering and unfair redistricting. Occasionally known as the most gerrymandered state, Texas (according to what we found) had one of its first instances of gerrymandering in 1981, with the Legislative Redistricting Board (LRB) proposing a map. Following this, “A consolidation of suits against the LRB's senate and house plans, Terrazas v. Clements, was filed in federal court in late 1981. The suit claimed that the senate plan violated the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments to the U.S. Constitution by diluting the voting strength of blacks, Hispanics, and Republicans and by ignoring communities of interest throughout the state.” (Council, n.d.). There are a large number of instances of gerrymandering that happened between 1981 and the current day; however, those are not the focus of this report.

Like Texas, Maryland isn’t immune to gerrymandering; however, Maryland has had significantly fewer instances of gerrymandering. One of the earliest (that we were able to find) was in 1966, where “the Eastern Shore was kept whole, but a large portion of Anne Arundel County, alongside all of Calvert and St. Mary’s counties, was added to the 1st District.” (Leckrone, 2021).

In more recent times, Texas and Maryland have proposed redistricting mid-decade. Texas voluntarily proposed and approved House Bill 4 (or HB4) in late August of 2025, which contained a new congressional map for the 38 districts. HB4 shifted 5 democrat districts towards republicans in comparison to the map enacted in 2021. On the other hand, Maryland did not approve a new map for 2026 because they were unable to come to an agreement on the proposed bill within the timeframe to redistrict a new map for their state. The 2022 map remains.

Our goal in finding new redistricting methods is to create more “fair” redistricting maps for Texas and Maryland, focused on maintaining proper proportions for accurate political and demographic representation, preserving existing political boundaries – for example, counties – and striving for compactness within our limitations. Some conflicts we’ve come across with these goals include geographical issues in Maryland, causing difficulties in code with contiguity and/or compactness. Other issues can involve dispersed communities and other political boundaries against proper district planning.

While there are several approaches to redistricting, some proposed ones by Shultzman (2020) include automated redistricting, where only algorithms are used to construct districts, minimizing subjective input or bias. However, this method has a setback of not accounting for local communities and boundaries created within regions, working strictly with numeric and geographical data aimed at balanced population, compactness, and contiguity. This also ignores potential influences such as racial and partisan information. If we were to combat this issue and focus specifically on electoral votes and political views, it could also cause problems with respect to what is fair for each party involved. Proportionality was introduced as a way for parties to win a fraction of districts similar to their statewide vote share, but this can often be difficult to achieve. Ultimately, what has been a relatively good measure against gerrymandering is evaluating compactness and matching partisan symmetry through seat votes.

Methods

        We first had to gather our sources; we used ALARM data, the PL94171 data, and data from the Redistricting Data Hub for our datasets and research.

So, we have the data we are going to use, but first, we need to understand how it was collected so we can make better judgments on what we find while using it. Starting with the PL94171 data, which was collected by the U.S. Census Bureau, they attempt to collect their data with a complete count (i.e., getting as close to 100% as possible) of every resident within the United States every 10 years that end with a zero. Both ALARM and Redistricting Data Hub appear to be using the Decennial Census data from the U.S. Census Bureau; the sampling used is the same method as before. Since the data was a complete count, it is as close to representative for the entire population within the U.S. as it possibly can be.

In order to create visualizations and compute metrics of the enacted 2025 Texas map, we had to combine data from three different sources. The 2025 district assignment data from Redistricting Data Hub used 15-digit GEOIDs, representing State+County+Tract+Block. The ALARM dataset had 2020 precinct-level election results, but its GEOIDs were 11-digit, so we could not join ALARM directly with the Redistricting Data Hub. However, the PL94171 map data filtered to sumlev = 750 level (block) had 15-digit GEOIDs that lined up with the Redistricting Data Hub. We combined the State (STATE), County (COUNTY), and Voting District (VTD) columns in PL94171 to determine the 11-digit GEOID that each 15-digit block fell within, then finally joined those 11-digit GEOIDs with the vote share data from ALARM to be able to get vote share statistics for the enacted 2025 Texas map.

Metrics:

We settled on several metrics to optimize in redistricting plans based on our fairness criteria. For reference, VAP refers to Voting Age Population, which is the population of people ages 18 and up. First, we compute several metrics forcriterionrness criteron to have proportional political representation in districts:

  • Proportion of districts that have a Republican voting majority: this metric is computed by summing the Republican votes (nrv) in each district assignment and summing the Democratic votes (ndv) in each district assignment, and calculating the fraction of districts that have more Republican votes than Democratic votes, in order to tell how many electoral votes the Republican party would gain in the state with this district plan. The vote share data is from ALARM’s 2020 election data.
  • Deviation from partisan symmetry: We represent the deviation from partisan symmetry by computing the excess Republican seat share above 50% when their vote share is 50%. For example, a 0.1 deviation from partisan symmetry means that in a 50-50 election, the plan allocates 60% of seats to the Republicans, rather than the symmetric outcome of 50% of seats. This measure is typically called the “partisan bias” of a plan in the academic literature on redistricting, and we compute this using the part_bias() function from ALARM’s redistricting metrics.
  • Partisan efficiency gap: The efficiency gap of a plan is the difference in the two parties’ wasted votes as a fraction of the statewide turnout. It is a measure of packing and cracking. If a Democrat won a district with 60% of the vote, then each vote above 50% + 1 of the total votes is wasted because the district could have won by 50% + 1 votes. Similarly, if a Democrat loses a district with 45%, then each of those votes are wasted because those votes did not lead to a seat. The efficiency gap is calculated as the Democratic wasted votes minus the Republican wasted votes. Thus, positive values indicate a pro-Republican bias, whereas negative values indicate a pro-Democratic bias. We compute this using the part_egap() function from ALARM’s redistricting metrics.

Second, there are several metrics for our fairness criterion to have proportional demographic representation in districts. We focus on the White, Hispanic, and Black demographics because these are the only demographics with enough population to form a majority in a district in Texas and Maryland.

  • Proportion of districts that have White VAP majority: this metric is computed by summing all Voting Age Population (VAP) members who are White in each district assignment and comparing them to the summed VAPs of other demographics in that district assignment, in order to find the fraction of districts that have a White majority with this district plan.
  • Proportion of districts that have a Hispanic VAP majority: this metric is computed the same way as the above metric, but with the Hispanic demographic.
  • Proportion of districts that have Black VAP majority: this metric is computed the same way as the above metric, but with the Black demographic.

Third, we use two metrics for our fairness criterion to have relatively compact districts. We compute the mean Polsby Popper and Reock compactness scores of all districts in a given plan:

  • Mean Polsby Popper compactness of districts: this metric measures the compactness of a district shape by comparing its area to a perfect circle with the same perimeter. Because it factors in the perimeter of the district, it is highly sensitive to jagged or winding shapes. We compute this using the comp_polsby() function from ALARM’s redistricting metrics, where an output of 0 is least compact, and 1 is most compact (a perfect circle).
  • Mean Reock compactness of districts: this metric measures the compactness of a district's shape by dividing the district's geographic area by the area of its minimum bounding circle (the smallest circle that completely encloses the district). Compared to Polsby Popper, Reock is less influenced by jagged shapes, but a downside is that a winding shape could still score high if it fits compactly inside one overall circle. We compute this using the comp_reock() function from ALARM’s redistricting metrics, where an output of 0 is least compact, and 1 is most compact (a perfect circle).

Finally, to assess our last fairness criteria of keeping existing political boundaries intact, we measure the number of county splits used to form the district shapes in a given redistricting plan. We compute this using the splits_total() function from ALARM’s redistricting metrics.

Regardless of any redistricting plan, we also compute the proportion of Republican votes out of all votes as a baseline of the state-wide Republican voter proportion. We also compute the proportion of White VAP out of all VAP, the proportion of Hispanic VAP out of all VAP, and the proportion of Black VAP out of all VAP as baseline state-wide demographics.

By computing these metrics for existing plans and newly generated maps, we hope to find redistricting plans that have a proportion of Republican-majority districts that is roughly equal to the state-wide Republican voter proportion, with partisan symmetry deviation and efficiency gap scores close to 0, with proportions of White-, Hispanic-, and Black-majority districts that are roughly equal to the state-wide VAP proportion of each demographic, while maximizing Polsby Popper and Reock compactness and minimizing the number of county splits to keep existing political boundaries intact. There are a lot of metrics to optimize here, so we expect there will be trade-offs in picking “fair” maps.

Texas redistricting methodology:

We perform redistricting on the state of Texas using ALARM’s redist_smc() function, which uses a Sequential Monte Carlo algorithm to generate district plans according to given constraints. We add a single Polsby Popper constraint with a strength of 1 to encourage compactness, then provide a parameter for counties that limits the algorithm to only generate maps that split up to 37 (the number of Texas districts minus 1) counties. We then set the algorithm to generate 1000 plans.

After generating these diverse plans, we identify the top candidate map that optimizes our fairness criteria metrics as best as possible. We considered splitting the state of Texas into several sectors and redistricting each individual sector to combine into the most optimal map, but it was not obvious what the best sectors in the state would be, so we opted for the redistricting algorithm on the state as a whole. We also decided to prioritize optimizing the proportional representation of the Hispanic VAP over other demographics because the most substantial minority group in Texas is Hispanic.

Maryland redistricting methodology:

Our Maryland redistricting process started with identifying our redistricting goals, which were to prioritize making contiguous districts that don’t cross geographical boundaries, keep communities together that are shaped by these geographical boundaries, and ensure a moderate number of districts that had a majority of minority voters. In Maryland, the most substantial minority group is black voters, with the highest concentrations around Baltimore and DC, the largest metropolitan areas in the state. Smaller concentrations of Hispanic and Asian voters are also present in subsets of these areas, but likely not large enough to constitute a majority presence in any district.

Another prioritization of ours in redistricting is compactness. This presents a substantial problem in Maryland, where the physical landscape and shape of the state provide a very low baseline of possible compactness for many of the districts in the state. The long, thin stretch in the upper west portion of the state must fall within the same district due to the low population density in that area, meaning that there will be an oblong-shaped district with a low compactness score, no matter what. The other significant issue posed by Maryland’s geography is the Chesapeake Bay. Chesapeake Bay is the largest estuary in the United States, and it happens to occupy a large portion of the state, with a section stretching 195 miles from the Maryland-Virginia border up into northeastern Maryland. This means over 1700 square miles of water with the shortest distances being 3-4 miles across that completely separates the areas of Maryland on the east and west coast. Since the expanse between the two sides is so vast and not easily crossable except for by ferries and the notable Chesapeake Bay Bridge that connects Annapolis to the eastern shore, this means that two distinct communities are formed on either side of the Bay. Following our goals of contiguity and keeping communities together, it would not make sense to create districts that cross the Chesapeake Bay.

When approaching redistricting in R, the packages use the precincts for each state to determine the spatial arrangement of the areas. The precincts cover the entire state, including water, meaning that the precincts for the east and west sides of Chesapeake Bay stretch into the bay itself to meet those on the other side. The arrangement of the precincts is determined by adjacencies, meaning each precinct has a list of several other precincts that it is positioned directly adjacent to, in each direction. In order to construct districts that don’t cross Chesapeake Bay, the adjacencies between the precincts directly on either side of the bay need to be removed. Below is a zoomed-in map of just the counties directly adjacent to Chesapeake Bay, with the adjacencies indicated by the red lines displaying each inter-precinct connection. Two objects were assigned in R that contained all the counties that directly bordered Chesapeake Bay on the east and the west shore, respectively. Then, a function was created that would take inputs of a dataset and those two objects and remove the adjacencies between them. This new dataset with the altered adjacencies was then used to simulate redistricting plans, using the redist_smc function, and generating 500 sample maps.

Discussion

Looking at the histograms in the Results section, it appears that the enacted 2021 and 2025 Texas maps may have been slightly gerrymandered because they have far more Republican majority districts than our generated maps, as well as having lower compactness and more county splits than our generated maps.

Based on the 1000 generated plans for Texas, we filtered our results by the plans that were closest to political proportionality in district representation. Since the ideal “fair” proportion of Republican voters out of all Texas voters was about 0.537, the closest proportion of a map we generated was 0.526. From here, since Hispanics are the most prevalent minority demographic in Texas, we decided to filter further to find plans with a proportion of Hispanic VAP majority districts that was closest to the state-wide proportion of Hispanic voters out of all Texas voters, which is 0.362. The closest value we found in our generated plans was a proportion of 0.368. We also filtered by plans that had at least 1 Black majority district to give them non-zero representation. From here, we sorted the remaining candidate plans to find those with the maximum Polsby Popper and Reock compactness scores. The most compact candidate plan had a mean Polsby Popper compactness of 0.294 and a mean Reock compactness of 0.447. It also happened to a plan with one of the lower numbers of county splits: 31 splits. The plan’s partisan symmetry score (-0.079) and partisan efficiency gap score (-0.018) were not zero, so the plan does have a slight Democratic bias. We still decided to go with this plan because plans with more even partisan symmetry and efficiency scores had lower compactness and more county splits, so this was a tradeoff in our fairness criteria.

Fig. 17: Most “fair” Texas map

Because we prioritized optimizing political and Hispanic demographic proportionality, as well as maximizing compactness and minimizing county splits, one tradeoff we made was in cracking some of the cities. The cities of Houston, San Antonio, and Dallas, in particular, look to be split up in a way that gives Democrats a majority in more districts than the enacted Texas plans. The negative partisan symmetry and partisan efficiency scores also indicate that some cracking occurred in this map.

From the 500 generated plans for Maryland, the selected map resulted in 0.25 majority black districts, falling just under the estimated proportion of Maryland’s total population at around 29-31%, and being on the higher end of proportions achieved by the maps. The map had a mean polsby compactness of 0.2475 and a mean reock compactness of 0.4064. Being combined with successfully generating districts that don’t cross Chesapeake Bay, this map meets our redistricting goals of keeping communities together, prioritizing compactness (as much as is feasible, given the geographical constraints of the state), and ensuring proportional representation for minority groups.

Fig. 21: Most “fair” Maryland map

Overall, our efforts into redistricting “fair” maps for Texas and Maryland have taught us that creating fair district plans is not an easy task and requires trade-offs. Attempting to maximize political and demographic proportionality and compactness is often done at the expense of cracking cities, in contrast to a more “neutral” approach that might only prioritize compactness and intactness of cities at the expense of giving less representation to certain parties or minority groups.
